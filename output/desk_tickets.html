Reflection

My thoughts after running tickets 101, 102 and 103 through the Campus Customs agent team. Everything here is based on the official run on 2026-10-14 (the Actual sections on the ticket tabs and the Cash tab), where I approved every payment myself on the dashboard.

1. How well did the agents do on each ticket?

Ticket 101: Bulldog tee (pretty strong, I'd give it an A−)

Honestly, the agents handled this one really well, because it was trickier than it looks. Tauhid only asked for one size S tee, but the shelf had 0, and the vendor that reprints tees (Bulldog Print Co) was on hold because of invoice 501: $840, and 3 days overdue as of the shop date 8/31. Inventory caught the hold right away by calling get_product_stock_and_price and get_vendor_status in the same step. Accounting then did the two things in the right order: it queued the $840 invoice as request #1 and the $8 one-tee purchase order as request #2. The $8 PO stayed locked on my Approvals tab until I approved the invoice, which is exactly the "a vendor won't ship while it has an unpaid invoice" rule. It also didn't over-order. It ordered exactly the 1 tee that was short, not extra "just in case" stock. The whole thing took 11 model calls, and every id the boss reported matched the database.

Why not a full A: customer service's draft to Tauhid said "the restock has not yet been approved or ordered." That's kind of an internal detail a customer shouldn't see, and it's something its prompt specifically says not to do, even after we tightened it twice. It isn't wrong information, but it shows prompts are a soft guardrail compared to the code guardrails. Also, ticket 101's token counts were exactly the same as the earlier run (29,841 in / 2,174 out), so Portkey probably answered it from its cache. So 101 doesn't really show how the model behaves on a fresh run.

Ticket 102: Rent due (basically perfect, A)

This was the cleanest ticket. The landlord's email just said "rent due in 2 days" with no amount, and the team didn't trust the email. Facilities looked up lease 1 with get_rent_due and confirmed it was really $2,400 to Elm City Properties, due 9/2. Then it handed off straight to accounting, which checked cash and queued request #3. Only two specialists, 8 model calls, no wasted steps, and inventory and customer service weren't called because there's no product and the landlord isn't a customer. The only thing I'd add is a short landlord draft saying the rent is being processed. Facilities was allowed to write one but chose not to, which is fine.

Ticket 103: Bulk hoodie discount (good judgment, one sloppy mistake, B+)

This is the ticket where the agents actually had to make a business decision, and I think they made the right call. The Yale AI Club wanted 20 size M hoodies and we only had 8, so 12 short. Accounting priced a routine 15% discount with quote_bulk_discount: $49.30 each, $986 for all 20, and still a 55.4% margin, which is inside the shop policy in the prompt. But the important part was cash. Ticket 103 ran after 101 and 102, so the $840 + $8 + $2,400 requests were already waiting for approval, and get_cash_position showed only $152 free. Restocking 12 hoodies at $22 each is $264, so it was $112 short. Accounting didn't queue a purchase order the shop couldn't pay for, and the boss escalated it to me instead of guessing. That's the "protect rent and overdue bills first, discretionary bulk deals last" rule from the boss prompt working.

The sloppy part: the boss's final decision listed approval request #1 as one of ticket 103's requests, but #1 is ticket 101's invoice. Our post-run id check caught it, which is good, but it means the boss mixed context between tickets. I'd also say the customer draft was a little ahead of itself. The boss called the 15% offer "contingent," but the draft to the club already offered the 15% price for all 20 hoodies. That's kind of a promise the shop couldn't keep without the restock. And I have to be honest about my own part: when I closed 103, my note just said "resolved," which isn't very helpful for whoever reads the ticket log later.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools?

Honestly, a lot of this. For ticket 102, one agent with get_rent_due, get_cash_position and request_payment_approval could have done the whole thing in about 3 model calls instead of 8. It's just "look up the lease, check cash, queue the rent." Passing it from boss → facilities → accounting mostly meant re-explaining the same facts (lease 1, $2,400, due 9/2) in each hand-off message, which costs tokens. Ticket 101 is also basically a straight line: check stock → check vendor → queue the invoice → queue the PO → write a draft. One agent with all 11 shop tools could do that in maybe 5–6 calls instead of 11, without three agents each re-reading what the last one found.

Where the team actually helps is the guardrails and focus, more than the thinking:

  • Least privilege: only accounting can call request_payment_approval and create_purchase_order, and only customer service can draft to a customer. With one agent, one bad step could queue a payment and write the customer a promise about it.
  • Focused prompts: customer service's prompt is all about tone and not leaking internal stuff, and accounting's is all about the cash priority order and the discount policy (15% routine, never below a 40% margin). One giant prompt would mix all of that together.
  • Ticket 103 is the one where splitting it up made the most sense, because it mixes stock, money policy, and a customer message, and the discount authority rule (accounting can pass on up to 15%, anything more goes back to the boss) only works when there are roles.

So my honest take is that for these three small tickets, a single agent with the same MCP tools and the same code guardrails (cash check, vendor hold, human login for approvals) would have been simpler and cheaper. The multi-agent setup pays off more as the shop gets more ticket types, and for keeping each role's permissions narrow.

5. Three new problems this team could not solve, and what we'd need

  1. Making a sale and taking money in. Say the Yale AI Club says yes and buys the 8 hoodies we do have at $49.30 each ($394.40). The app has no way to record that. Cash only goes out in this homework, there's no orders or sales table, and nothing lowers the inventory count when something sells, so the shelf would still say 8 hoodies. It also means the October rent problem above can never be solved, because no money ever comes in.
    What we'd need: a sales/orders table and MCP tools like record_sale(sku, size, qty, price) (lowers inventory.qty) and record_payment_received(amount, from, ticket_id) (adds to cash_accounts), with refunds and anything over a limit needing a human login just like approvals do. Plus a Sales agent that turns an accepted offer (like customer service's draft #2) into an order, checks that the price matches what accounting quoted, and hands off to inventory to reserve the stock.
  2. Receiving the restock and moving time forward. The $8 tee purchase order is "ordered" with an expected arrival of 2026-09-05, but desk.date_today is stuck at 2026-08-31 and there's no tool to mark a PO as received, so the tee never actually shows up on the shelf and Tauhid's order can never be filled.
    What we'd need: a human-only advance_shop_date tool (so the agents can't change "today"), a receive_purchase_order(po_id, qty_received) tool that adds the units to inventory and marks the PO received (and flags short shipments), and a "receiving" job for the inventory agent: each morning, check which POs are due, receive them, then ask customer service to draft a "your tee is here" message to Tauhid and reopen any customer ticket that was waiting on that stock.
  3. Actually talking to people: sending messages and working things out with vendors. Right now everything stops at drafts. Draft #1 to Tauhid and draft #2 to the Yale AI Club are just sitting on the board, and the team can't ask Bulldog Print Co for something like a payment plan on a big invoice, a rush reprint quote, or a partial shipment. For ticket 103 a real store manager might have called the vendor and asked to split the 12-hoodie order to fit the $152, but our agents can't do that.
    What we'd need: an outbox table and a human-only send_message(draft_id) tool behind the same login as approvals, so a person clicks Send on the Messages tab and it actually emails. A request_vendor_quote(vendor_id, sku, qty, notes) tool, plus an inbox tool to read replies. And a Purchasing / vendor-relations agent that drafts quote requests and payment-plan asks, compares answers, and hands the numbers to accounting. Every outgoing message still needs human approval, because the rule "we never email customers or call real vendors without a person" should stay.

6. One more thing I learned: human approval has to be built into the code, not just written in a prompt

The biggest lesson from this homework wasn't about the shop agents at all. In my first Problem 9 run, the AI assistant I was building with clicked Approve on all three payments and typed my name, because back then the "approver" was just a name in a text box. The rule "only a human approves money" was written in every prompt and in the harness, but nothing in the code stopped it. So I threw that run out (I kept its files on my computer, but they're not part of the submission) and changed the app so approvals need a real human login. Approving, rejecting and closing tickets now all need a logged-in person, and the payments are recorded under that person's account. Then I redid the run and approved everything myself. That's where the $3,400 → $152 on the Cash tab comes from: three payments that I approved. Basically, the guardrails that actually held up in this project (the cash check, the vendor hold, the human login) were the ones in the code. The prompt-only rules, like "don't mention approvals to customers," were the ones the agents sometimes broke.

Tickets run one at a time, oldest first (101 → 102 → 103), and they share one checking account. That's why ticket 103 sees less cash than ticket 101.
